Updated June 7, 2026. Browser extensions can be useful, but they sit close to searches, shopping, email, passwords, school accounts, work apps, and banking sessions. A household review is not about panic-removing everything; it is about knowing what is installed, what permissions are requested, who uses each profile, and what to do if an add-on becomes suspicious.

Security note: do not paste passwords, recovery codes, private screenshots, or financial details into extension support forms or unofficial chats.

![Household browser extension safety review](/images/illustrations/sbg-extension-permission-20260607-hero.png)

| Permission or signal | Why it matters | Safer action |
|---|---|---|
| Read/change site data | Can affect sensitive pages | Allow only when purpose is clear |
| Unknown publisher | Harder to trust maintenance | Prefer known vendors and official stores |
| No longer used | Risk without benefit | Remove it |
| Child or senior profile | Higher support risk | Keep only essential add-ons |
| Work account browser | Employer policy may apply | Use approved profile/device rules |

## Inventory before judging
Open each browser profile and list installed extensions, publisher, last remembered purpose, and whether anyone still uses it. Households often find old coupon tools, PDF helpers, meeting add-ons, school tools, and abandoned utilities. If nobody can explain why it is present, removal is usually safer.

![Extension review with blank notes](/images/illustrations/sbg-extension-permission-20260607-01.png)

## Match permissions to a real purpose
A password manager, grammar tool, shopping helper, and screen recorder do not need the same access. Broad permissions may be legitimate for some tools, but the household should know why. Be careful with extensions that can read or change data on all sites.

![Parent and teen discussing laptop safety](/images/illustrations/sbg-extension-permission-20260607-02.png)

## Protect separate profiles
Shared computers become safer when profiles are separated. Children may add school or entertainment tools without understanding permissions; seniors may keep extensions installed after a support call; work accounts may have policy requirements.

![Privacy cleanup desk](/images/illustrations/sbg-extension-permission-20260607-03.png)

## Use removal and recovery steps, not fear
If an extension looks suspicious, remove it, restart the browser, check passwords used in that profile, review recent sign-ins for important accounts, and run browser or operating system security checks. If money, identity, or work data may be involved, preserve notes and use official recovery or reporting channels.

![Recovery scene with blank devices](/images/illustrations/sbg-extension-permission-20260607-04.png)

## Practical checklist
- Review every browser profile, not just the main one.
- Remove extensions nobody recognizes or uses.
- Treat broad site-data permissions as a deliberate trust decision.
- Keep work, child, and shared profiles separated where possible.
- If suspicious activity appears, remove, restart, review sign-ins, and document steps.

## Mistakes that weaken the plan
| Mistake | Security risk | Better habit |
|---|---|---|
| Keeping old coupon tools forever | Unnecessary access remains | Remove unused add-ons |
| Using one shared profile | Permissions and history mix | Separate profiles |
| Trusting search-ad support pages | Fake support paths can mislead | Use official browser menus |
| Ignoring work policy | Data exposure risk | Use approved device rules |

## FAQ
### Are all browser extensions dangerous?
No. The risk is unmanaged access. Keep useful extensions from trusted sources, remove stale tools, and understand broad permissions.

### What is the fastest first step?
Remove extensions no one recognizes or has used recently, then review important account sign-ins.
